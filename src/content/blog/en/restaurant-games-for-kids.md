The order is in, the food is twenty minutes away, and a phone is already sliding across the table. Here are four quiet games that fit on one table, use nothing you don’t already have, and still work for kids who think they’ve seen it all. We’ve tested each one with a real 11-year-old.

## Three rules that make table games work

- **Ask before you explain.** Let your child guess first. The science only sticks once they’ve been wrong about it.
- **Keep it quiet and on the table.** Empty glasses only, nothing that flies, nothing that spills.
- **Stop when the food arrives.** If the conversation carries on while you eat, it worked.

## 1. Floating forks (10 minutes)

**You need:** two forks, one toothpick, one empty glass.

1. Push the prongs of the two forks into each other so the handles form a V.
2. Slide the toothpick through the gap between the prongs, where the forks cross.
3. Rest the tip of the toothpick on the rim of the glass and adjust slowly until it stays.

The forks hang off the glass as if they were floating. Before you let go, ask: *“Where is the balance point? Point to it.”* Most kids point at the forks. The real answer is in the air, just below the rim.

**Why it works:** the heavy handles curve back underneath the toothpick, so the balance point of the whole shape sits below the point that holds it up. When the balance point hangs below the support, the shape swings back instead of falling.

**Level up:** let only the very tip of the toothpick touch the rim, then try balancing it on a fingertip with no glass at all.

> Try it at home first. Fork shapes vary a lot, and a restaurant is the worst place to discover yours won’t balance.

## 2. The chopstick quiz (Chinese restaurants)

Read each question aloud and let everyone guess before you reveal the answer.

- **How old are the oldest chopsticks ever found?** About 3,000 years. Bronze chopsticks were dug up at the ruins of Yin, near Anyang in China.
- **What were they first used for?** Mostly cooking: reaching into hot pots. Eating with them became common much later, around 2,000 years ago.
- **Why are they called *kuàizi* in Chinese?** A popular story says boatmen about 500 years ago avoided the old word *zhù*, because it sounds like “stop”. They said *kuài*, “fast”, instead.

Finish with two table-manner puzzles: why shouldn’t chopsticks stand upright in a bowl of rice? (They look like incense sticks placed for people who have died.) And why not point with them? (Pointing at people is rude, chopsticks or not.)

## 3. Menu detective (any restaurant in Europe)

In the EU, restaurants must be able to tell you whether a dish contains any of 14 allergens: cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphites, lupin and molluscs.

The game: find out how this menu marks them (numbers, letters, symbols, or “ask your server”), guess which ones are in your order, then check.

It opens a good conversation. An **allergy** is the body’s defence system mistaking a harmless part of a food for an invader and overreacting. An **intolerance** is different: some people’s stomachs struggle to digest something, like the sugar in milk, which is uncomfortable but doesn’t involve the defence system. Close with: *“If a friend with a nut allergy came with us, what would we ask the waiter?”*

This is curiosity, not medical advice. If your child has an allergy, follow your own doctor’s plan.

## 4. Twenty questions, the smart way

Everyone knows twenty questions. Here’s the twist that makes it interesting at 11: talk about *which* questions are good.

A great yes-or-no question rules out about half of what’s left. Do that twenty times and you can tell apart roughly a million possibilities (2 × 2 × 2… twenty times is 1,048,576). A weak question, like “Is it a spoon?”, rules out almost nothing.

Play it in reverse: your child thinks of something in the restaurant, you ask. Afterwards, ask them which of your questions helped most, and which one you wasted.
