Most “science experiments for kids” online are aimed at six-year-olds, or need a shopping trip. These five use things you already have, and each one passed our three-part test before it made the list:

1. It works within ten minutes, first time.
2. When it fails, kids laugh instead of giving up.
3. The science fits in one true sentence.

For each one: let your child **predict first**, then build, then explain. The prediction is what makes the explanation land.

## 1. The one-sheet paper bridge

**You need:** two paper cups (or two books), one sheet of A4 paper, ten coins.

Put the cups upside down about a hand’s width apart and lay the flat sheet across. Add one coin: it sags straight away. Now fold the same sheet into a zigzag along its long side, with folds about a finger wide, and lay it across again. Add coins one at a time.

**Ask first:** flat or folded, which will hold more?

**The science:** folding doesn’t add any material; it changes the shape. Every fold becomes a small wall standing on its edge, and a wall on its edge is very hard to bend. Corrugated cardboard and metal roof sheets use the same trick.

**Level up:** double the gap, or try with half a sheet.

## 2. Paper columns that hold up books

**You need:** three sheets of paper, a little tape, a stack of books.

Roll one sheet into a round tube, fold one into a square tube and one into a triangular tube, all the same height. Stand each on the table and add books on top, one by one, until it buckles.

**Ask first:** which shape will hold the most?

**The science:** columns usually fail at their weakest spot. Corners and flat faces give the paper a place to buckle first; a round tube spreads the load evenly, so it usually lasts longest. That’s one reason pillars are so often round.

## 3. The coin that won’t move

**You need:** a stiff card (a playing card works), a coin, an empty glass.

Put the card on top of the glass and the coin in the middle of the card. Flick the card sideways, hard and fast. The coin drops straight into the glass. Then try pulling the card slowly: the coin comes along with it.

**Ask first:** fast or slow, which will get the coin into the glass?

**The science:** objects that are still tend to stay still. A fast flick moves the card away before friction has time to drag the coin along; with nothing under it, the coin simply falls. Magicians use the same idea to pull a tablecloth from under a set table (please don’t).

## 4. The ketchup-packet diver

**You need:** a clear plastic bottle with a cap, water, a sealed sauce packet.

First drop the packet into a glass of water: you want one that only just floats. Put it into the full bottle, screw the cap on tight, and squeeze. The packet sinks. Let go, and it rises again.

**Ask first:** what will happen to the packet when you squeeze the bottle?

**The science:** there’s a small bubble of air inside the packet. Squeezing the bottle raises the pressure and squashes that bubble, so the packet takes up less space for the same weight. Less water is pushed aside, the upward push gets smaller, and it sinks.

**Level up:** can you make it hover exactly halfway down?

## 5. The reaction-time ruler

**You need:** a 30 cm ruler.

Hold the ruler at the top end. Your child waits with thumb and finger open around the zero mark, not touching it. Drop it without warning; they catch it as fast as they can. Read the number where their fingers are.

Because a falling object speeds up at a known rate, the distance tells you the time: *t* = √(2*d* ÷ *g*), where *g* is about 9.81 m/s².

| Caught at | Reaction time |
|---|---|
| 5 cm | 0.10 s |
| 10 cm | 0.14 s |
| 15 cm | 0.17 s |
| 20 cm | 0.20 s |
| 25 cm | 0.23 s |
| 30 cm | 0.25 s |

**Level up:** compare dominant and other hand, or test while they are chatting. Distraction usually slows everyone down.
